# Running the review

The wireframe is the instrument. This is how you use it. Paste it into a doc and hand it to whoever is facilitating.

---

## Before the session

Have ready:

- The prototype running, wireframe mode on, toggle reachable from every screen.
- Two or three real tasks, written as an outcome rather than a route. "Issue a credential to a supplier" is a task. "Click the button in the top right" is a script.
- A way to record. Screen recording is enough; you are counting hesitations, not coding transcripts.

Do not send the wireframe round in advance. Reactions to a still image are reactions to an image. What you want is someone attempting something.

---

## During

Start in wireframe mode. Say one line about why it looks like that and then stop explaining:

> "The visual design is switched off so we can look at how it works. It comes back at the end."

Give the first task. Then be quiet. The instinct to help is strong and every intervention costs you the finding.

Write down, in this order of usefulness:

1. **Where they stop.** A pause before a click is the product failing to make something obvious. Note what they were looking for.
2. **Where they go wrong.** Which thing did they try instead, and what made it look right?
3. **What they ask.** "Is this a button?" and "what does this bit do?" are both structural failures.
4. **What they narrate.** People read the screen aloud when they are unsure. The words they use are the words the interface should have used.
5. **What they never see.** Elements that were never looked at, never mentioned, never used. On a coloured screen these often get noticed because of a tint. Under grey, they disappear, which tells you what the tint was propping up.

Resist the two conversations that will try to start:

- *"Is this what it's going to look like?"* No. It comes back at the end. Move on.
- *"Shouldn't that be a different colour?"* Note it, do not discuss it. Colour questions in a wireframe review are the reflex you removed the colour to avoid.

---

## At the end

Switch to the styled version. Ask one question:

> "Anything you got stuck on that looks sorted here?"

The answer is the point of the whole exercise. If a participant says yes, the styling was compensating for a structural problem. The compensation works right up until the user is tired, on a phone, in a hurry, colour-blind, or looking at the third screen rather than the first. The problem is still there, painted over.

If the answer is no, the structure is doing its job and the visual design is free to be a visual design rather than a repair.

---

## Writing it up

Keep it to one page and keep it descriptive. Findings, not fixes; the team decides the fixes.

```
Screen: [name]
What happened: [observed behaviour, no interpretation]
What it suggests: [one sentence]
Confidence: [how many participants, or "single observation"]
```

Single observations are worth recording and worth labelling as single observations. Two people hesitating in the same place is a pattern. One person hesitating once might be the person.

---

## The three findings that come up most

Worth knowing in advance, because you will probably see them.

**No primary action.** Once everything interactive is the same blue, a screen with one clear next step looks completely different from a screen with fourteen equal options. Most early prototypes are the second kind. The fix is hierarchy, not colour.

**Meaning carried by colour alone.** A red badge, a green tick, an amber row. In greyscale they say nothing. Add a label, an icon with a distinct shape, or a position that means something. Colour should confirm meaning, not supply it.

**Screens carrying more than one idea.** A shared background colour can hold two unrelated things together convincingly. Remove it and they separate. Usually the right answer is two screens, or one screen with a real container and a real heading.
